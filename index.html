Why this exists

Municipal elections throw off a lot of information in a short time. Candidates make promises, residents ask questions, reporters dig, people organize, and institutions issue statements. In Kenora this year it landed in newspapers, council documents, podcasts, Facebook threads and videos, and much of it is hard to find twice.

This year's race also turned into a conversation about reconciliation, homelessness, policing, spending and what we expect from the people we elect. Not everyone has the time to dig through all of that, or to know which parts are council's business and which belong to other governments. So a few of us collected it in one place.

We tried to hold ourselves to some simple rules. A complaint isn't a finding. Running together isn't proof of a voting deal. A controversial statement isn't automatically a broken law. We quote people's own words, link the original, and leave the judging to you.

We're also keeping it because elections end and posts disappear. Democracy mostly happens between elections, in council chambers, community meetings and the work of keeping track. This is part of the keeping track.

Land acknowledgement

Kenora is situated within Treaty #3 territory, on the traditional lands and waters of the Anishinaabe Nation. We recognize the continued presence, governance, inherent rights and leadership of Anishinaabe peoples, alongside the contributions of Métis people and other Indigenous residents who call this region home.

The history of this municipality can't be separated from treaty relationships, colonization, residential schools, displacement or the continued work of Indigenous peoples to protect their lands, communities and futures.

School board trustees

School boards cover the whole region, and each board's trustee seats are split among the communities it serves. On this ballot you're voting only for the trustees who represent the City of Kenora.

Keewatin-Patricia District School Board (public): Kenora elects three trustees, and four people are running: Anita Cameron, David Cornish, Grant Erickson and Rory McMillan. Cameron and McMillan have profiles on the City's site.

Catholic and French-language boards: Kenora's trustees on these boards were acclaimed, so there's no vote.

You vote for trustees on the board you support. If you're not sure which one, call the voter help line at 807-470-8620.

04Ask them this

At the door, at the rink, in the comments, and at council after the vote. These work whoever you support. A real answer has a number, a plan or a “no” in it.

Campus of Care and health care

  • Do you support the Campus of Care and the All Nations Hospital? What would the City contribute, and what's up to the province?
  • How will the First Nations whose members travel to Kenora for care have a say in the planning?

Anicinabe Park and Treaty #3

  • Three First Nations are seeking the return of Anicinabe Park, and their claim against the City and Canada is going ahead. Would you push the City to negotiate rather than keep defending in court? What would a fair outcome look like to the Nations?
  • Treaty #3 Nations are governments, not stakeholders. How would you work with them government to government?
  • Where Treaty #3 Nations and the City both have a stake, like land use, policing and the lake, how would you make decisions together rather than consult after the fact?

Community safety

  • When someone's in crisis downtown, who should respond: police, bylaw, outreach or a crisis team? What would you fund?
  • The City's policing study priced a Kenora police force at $13.2 to $15.9 million a year, roughly double the OPP. Still want one? Which taxes pay for it?
  • Mental health and wellness calls are rising while Criminal Code calls decline. Are more officers the answer to those calls?
  • The outgoing council backed hiring Community Safety Officers but left the cost and mandate to the next council. What would they enforce, against whom, and at what cost?
  • Have you read Kenora's Community Safety and Well-Being Plan? What would you do with it?

Homelessness and housing

  • What's the City's job here, and what belongs to KDSB, the province and the federal government?
  • The Ontario Human Rights Commission reported on homelessness in Kenora and warned council against an anti-loitering bylaw, which council voted down. Would you support bylaws aimed at people who are unhoused downtown?
  • What would you do on zoning and development to get more homes built?

Reconciliation

  • Do you accept the findings of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission?
  • Which TRC Calls to Action aimed at municipalities would you act on first? (See Civics 101.)
  • What's your position on harm reduction, and what evidence is it based on?

Money and infrastructure

  • The budget is the new council's first big job. How would you pay for what you're promising, and what would you cut?
  • Water rates, roads like Coker Road, the Portage Bay bridge: what gets fixed first?
  • How do you grow year-round jobs and tourism, and protect rinks, trails, parks and programs?

The TRC, Anicinabe Park, drug policy, CSWB plan and inclusion questions were posted for every candidate in an online forum on Sept 16, after being emailed to Treadway, Johnson and McCaffrey. McCaffrey replied and said he accepts the TRC's findings. Johnson's answers arrived after the deadline through a third party. Treadway declined.

Anicinabe Park, 2024–25“Grifters, pure and simple”

On June 25, 2024, a 57-year-old man died in an officer-involved shooting at Anicinabe Park, and the Special Investigations Unit opened an investigation. At council on July 16, Bernie read a statement saying he spoke for himself, not the OPP or the City. He called much of the public discussion “subjective claptrap” and said it came from “the media, politicians and community leaders or those who call themselves community leaders, who are in my mind, nothing more than grifters, pure and simple, whose livelihood depends on perpetuating the victim narrative.” He ended that passage with “Shame on all of you!”

A resident complained that the remarks breached council's code of conduct. Weighing freedom of political expression, the Integrity Commissioner found no violation. His words are transcribed from the meeting recording in the report.

His response to being cleared, at council: “Hurt feelings don't matter. They certainly don't matter to me.”

Homelessness, January 2025“People choose to be in Kenora”

At a Jan 14, 2025 committee of the whole meeting, he told KDSB CAO Henry Wall he believes homelessness is “a choice,” the Miner & News reported. His words: “People choose to be in Kenora, they choose to couch surf, they choose to be at the shelter, they choose to be in encampments.” He also suggested more housing be built in First Nation communities.

The evidence: KDSB's October 2024 count, reported in the same story, found 325 people experiencing homelessness in Kenora, nearly triple 2021. Of 249 surveyed, 163 said they want housing, and low income was the top barrier. Across the wider district, KDSB counted 500 people.

Policing, 2025–26The big idea, priced

He moved a study budgeted at about $95,000 on bringing back a city police force. It found a Kenora force would cost roughly double the OPP. He then proposed City-hired Community Safety Officers for downtown, with costs left to the next council.

The other complaint against him this term alleged a conflict of interest over ice time for the Kenora Islanders. He denied it, and the Integrity Commissioner found it unfounded in March 2025. Neither complaint ended in a finding of misconduct.

06Running together

Kenora council races usually run on individual names. This year Tyler Johnson, Chris McCaffrey and Karla Treadway are campaigning together as A New Vision for Kenora, which the Miner & News calls a voting bloc.

Do the math. Council has seven votes, the mayor plus six councillors. Four carries a motion. A bloc of three needs just one more.

On Sept 6, a voter asked Treadway on Facebook who else shares her vision. She answered: “Tyler Johnson, Chris McCaffrey and Bob Bernie — check out @anewvisionforkenora on fb.” That's her naming Bernie, in her words. It doesn't prove he joined the group or agreed to vote with them.

Same phrase, weeks apart

July 27, 2026 · PodcastKarla Treadway

Episode of The Sovereign Sphere: “Canada's Suicidal Empathy: The Paradox That's Killing a Nation.” Guest: Phillip Millar.

Aug 19, 2026 · Letter to the editorBob Bernie

“Suicidal empathy is destroying our community.” “Suicidal empathy has replaced rational thought and in fact reality in our community… Everyone is not a victim. The ‘victimhood industry’ is excusing bad behaviour in our community.” It names harm reduction, safe injection sites, needle exchanges and low-barrier shelters as part of the problem.

Where the phrase comes from. “Suicidal empathy” is the title of a 2026 bestseller by Gad Saad, a Canadian marketing professor. It isn't a recognized psychological diagnosis or research finding. Wikipedia calls it a “purported phenomenon,” and writer Cathy Young has called it “a right-wing buzzword.” In Bernie's letter, it's used to argue against harm reduction: the services that keep people alive long enough to reach treatment.

On Sept 17, answering the Miner & News Q&A on services and spending, Treadway wrote: “I'd fund and accelerate the Community Safety Officer model that Bob Bernie has already been pushing council to consider.”

Shared language isn't proof of a deal, and we're not claiming one. It's worth knowing where the ideas in this election come from.

08Indigenous leadership

Anishinaabe Nations, leaders, Elders and organizers shaped this election through governance, land claims, organizing and ceremony, not only by answering what candidates said. Each speaks for themselves or for the Nation or organization they represent. No single voice here speaks for every Indigenous person or every Nation in Treaty #3.

Grand Council Treaty #3

“Not up for debate in a political platform.”

Ogichidaa Francis Kavanaugh, Sept 16, on the dignity, rights and jurisdiction of the Anishinaabe Nation

Grand Council doesn't tell its citizens how to vote. The Ogichidaa asked everyone to get informed, vote, and remember the weight of the words we use as neighbours and treaty partners. Read the statement.

Anicinabe Park: land, treaty and jurisdiction

Niisaachewan Anishinaabe Nation, Wauzhushk Onigum Nation and Obashkaandagaang are suing Canada and the City of Kenora for the return of Anicinabe Park. They say the Crown bought the land in 1929 and treated it as reserve land, then sold it to the City in 1959 without their consultation or consent. The Nations describe it as a gathering place used for thousands of years for feasts, trade, fishing and ceremony, and a place where families stayed to visit their children at residential school.

The return of the land was also a demand of the 1974 occupation, when the Ojibway Warriors Society held the park for about six weeks. In July 2025, an Ontario court refused to dismiss the Nations' claim on time-limit grounds, so it goes ahead. All sides say they would prefer a negotiated settlement. The City says it will defend the claim in the meantime.

“Reconciliation starts with returning Anicinabe Park.”

Niisaachewan Anishinaabe Nation

This is a question of treaty rights and land, and the Nations are the ones making the case. Council's part is real, though: the City is a defendant, so council decides whether to negotiate and how the park is run while the claim is in court.

09Civics 101

Campaign promises are cheap. The law decides what a councillor can actually do. Here's the short version of the rules they'll be working under.

Municipal Act, 2001, s. 224What a councillor's job is

Represent the public, decide what services the City provides, develop policy, and make sure the City is accountable and transparent.

So: “I'll fix crime” isn't a council power. Budgets, bylaws and services are.

Municipal Act, Part VI.1Strong mayor powers

Kenora's mayor has held them since May 1, 2025. The mayor proposes the budget, can veto some bylaws, hires the CAO, and can pass certain bylaws with one-third of council.

So: a two-thirds council vote overrides a veto. That's five of seven. Who you elect still matters.

Municipal Accountability Act, 2025 (Bill 9)New conduct rules

Royal Assent June 2, 2026. A single provincial code of conduct replaces local ones, the provincial Integrity Commissioner investigates serious misconduct, and there's a process to remove a councillor. Its main sections take effect on dates the province sets, not all at once.

So: this council may be the first held to the new standard, depending on when it's switched on.

Municipal Elections Act, 1996Third-party advertisers

Paid advertising that promotes or opposes candidates, run by someone other than a candidate, is “third-party advertising.” Those advertisers must register with the Clerk and follow donation and spending rules. Everyday unpaid political talk, like posting your opinion online, is a different thing.

So: Rock the Indigenous Vote registered and plays by those rules. Read the Act.

Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019Safety plans are the law

Ontario requires every municipality to have a Community Safety and Well-Being Plan built with health, social service and Indigenous partners. Kenora has one.

So: “nobody's doing anything” usually means “I haven't read the plan.”

Ontario Human Rights CodeServices are for everyone

The City can't deliver services or enforce bylaws in ways that discriminate, including against people because they're Indigenous, disabled or receiving public assistance.

So: the OHRC has already warned Kenora once. See Ask them this.

TRC Calls to Action 43, 47, 57, 75, 77Municipalities are named

Adopt the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (43). Reject the Doctrine of Discovery (47). Train public servants in Indigenous history and treaties (57). Protect residential school cemeteries and burial sites (75). Share records with the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation (77).

So: reconciliation isn't a federal-only file. Read the Calls.

Municipal Conflict of Interest ActMoney conflicts

A councillor with a financial interest in a matter has to declare it and stay out of the discussion and the vote. Separately, the Code of Conduct covers behaviour. Anyone can complain to the Integrity Commissioner, who investigates and reports.

So: disagreeing with a councillor, criticizing them, filing a complaint and having a complaint upheld are four different things.

Four ways to read this election

Like a political scientist

Seven votes, one bloc of three. Turnout in 2022 was 54.6%. Low turnout makes organized blocs more powerful.

Like an accountant

Every promise has a cost and a line in the budget. Ask which line, and what gets cut to pay for it.

Like a historian

Kenora sits on Treaty #3 land and was home to Cecilia Jeffrey Indian Residential School. The TRC's findings are the public record. Questioning a record isn't the same as having evidence against it.

Like a social worker

The City's policing study shows calls about mental health and wellness rising. Problems that are about health need health responses. That's the idea behind Ontario's community safety and well-being planning: prevention before policing.

13How we did this

  • Sources. Official City records, Integrity Commissioner reports, legislation, candidates' own profiles, letters, questionnaire answers and podcasts, and published reporting. Where several outlets ran the same story, we list the original and note the reprints. A reprint isn't a second source.
  • Quotes. Quotation marks mean we checked the wording against the original document, recording transcript or article. Everything else is a summary, and the link is there so you can check it.
  • Allegations and findings. We say who complained, what was investigated, what the investigator found, and what council did after. A complaint is never presented as proof.
  • Privacy. Private residents aren't named unless they're public figures in this story. Names in screenshots are blurred.
  • Indigenous voices. We quote Nations, leaders and organizers in their own words and link to their own statements wherever they exist, so their sources come first. Each speaks for themselves or the body they represent, not for every Indigenous person. This guide was put together by residents, not by a First Nation or Indigenous organization. Anyone named here, and any Treaty #3 Nation, can ask us to correct or remove what we've written about them.
  • Corrections. If we got something wrong, tell us and we'll fix it and note the change.
